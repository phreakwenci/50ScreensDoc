## Short film summary / synopsis
At its core, 50 Screens is a national portrait told through local stories. Each theater
represents a different facet of the American experience: a single-screen cinema in a
rural farming town struggling to stay open after the pandemic; a historic downtown
theater fighting rising rents in a gentrifying city; a community-run nonprofit cinema
redefining what moviegoing can be; and a multi-generational family business confronting
whether the next generation will continue the legacy. The film unfolds as a road journey
— episodic yet cumulative — building toward an understanding of what is being lost and
what is still worth saving. Rather than focusing solely on decline, it seeks the tension
between crisis and reinvention. "Can independent theaters survive — and if so, what will
they become?" The answer is not predetermined. 50 Screens invites the audience into the
uncertainty.

## Filmmaker statement (Jan-David Soutar, first person)
When I was nineteen, I was going through chemotherapy and got a job at a local mom-and-pop
movie theater. I interviewed with a full head of hair; a week later, on my first day, it
was gone. That family never batted an eye — they helped me through one of the hardest
experiences of my life. That theater taught me something I have carried ever since: movie
theaters are places where people gather, reflect, and find connection.

My creative process begins with listening, building trust, and allowing people to reveal
the emotional truths beneath the surface of a story. I am drawn to communities,
institutions, and traditions that are overlooked until they begin to disappear. My work
sits at the intersection of narrative, documentary, and live experience, and I am always
thinking about the relationship between a film and the audience encountering it — work
that invites participation rather than passive consumption.

50 Screens is an evolution of that practice. It combines my interests in filmmaking,
exhibition, and community building into a single project. For me, the making of the film
and the future life of the film are deeply connected. Success as an artist is not fame;
it is creating work that endures in the minds of audiences and strengthens the
communities it touches. The greatest challenge I face is scale: building relationships
across the country while developing the creative, financial, and logistical
infrastructure an ambitious documentary requires.

## Artistic vision / style
The visual language is rooted in the observational tradition of cinéma vérité. The camera
is a witness, not a participant. There is no narrator and no on-screen director. The film
earns its authority by disappearing into the spaces it documents — a direct inheritance
from Barbara Kopple's *Harlan County USA* and its radical patience: staying still long
enough for the truth to show up. The crew is intentionally lean, two to three people on
interview days: a Sony FX3 in 4K, natural and mixed ambient light, classic single-subject
interview grammar, medium and close framing, and a second camera for reaction and
context. The aesthetic goal is intimacy over polish. These are not people who want to
perform; they want to be heard, and the camera's job is to create the conditions for
that. The film will look like the spaces it inhabits: worn, specific, full of texture —
the light through a projection booth window, the velvet of a seat that has held thousands
of people, the face of an owner who has run out of options but not out of reasons to stay
open. Sound is a first-class creative element: projector hum, reel changes, the echo of
an empty auditorium, the crowd noise of a full house. The original score gives those
ambient sounds room to breathe. The film is built in three movements — anchor theaters
(MI, MD, GA, CA); regional cluster shoots across all fifty states; and a close at
CinemaCon, where studio spectacle meets the small theaters we have been documenting. The
question at the center is not *whether* independent theaters survive — some will, some
won't — but **what survival costs, and who pays the price** when a community institution
has to change its identity to keep existing. The film will not pretend there is a clean
answer.

## Ethics & accountability  ⭐ frame as filmmaker–subject relationship, not logistics
> **Playbook rule:** this is about the ethical relationship with subjects — ongoing
> consent, transparency, control over sensitive material — not door signage or set safety.

Accountability to our subjects is central. Interviewees — patrons, staff, owners, council
members, scholars — are treated as partners contributing to positive change, not subjects
on a screen. Interview questions are shared and vetted with subjects in advance. Many will
discuss genuinely sensitive material: family memories and personal loss, financial
instability, burnout, and the uncertain future of a business they have given their lives
to. If a subject wishes to discuss something off camera, we work with them — using audio
with other visual storytelling in post. Consent is ongoing, not a one-time release: we are
transparent about how footage will be used and give subjects a way to raise concerns
before the cut is locked. For filming the public inside theaters, we coordinate with
management in advance so patrons are informed and any concerns addressed.

## Community care & safety
> **Playbook rule:** answer the *emotional/reputational* wellbeing question, not just
> physical safety.

Our community-care framework protects the emotional and reputational wellbeing of people
sharing vulnerability on camera — financial hardship, family conflict, the possibility of
losing their business. We manage each subject's relationship to the film through
post-production, keep them informed of how their story is being used, and honor a
subject's request to retract sensitive material before picture lock. Physical set safety
(clear call sheets, safe locations, adequate crew) is a baseline we also maintain, but the
heart of our care practice is trust with the people on camera.

## Audience  ⭐ include the theaters' own patrons
Primary audience: adults who grew up going to neighborhood theaters — Baby Boomers and
Gen X at the core — designed to also reach Gen Z audiences who never had access to a
family-owned theater and may not know what they are missing. Beyond nostalgia, the film
speaks to younger cinephiles, film students, arts-community members, and local advocates
who care about what their communities lose. Crucially, **every theater we profile has an
existing audience**, and each becomes a constituency for the film when it screens there.
That is audience development and distribution in a single move: the Nouveau Roadshow
network is built from this audience outward.

## Distribution & marketing  ⭐ must name Nouveau Roadshow
The theatrical release is self-distributed through **Nouveau Roadshow's partner theater
network** — a theatrical-only model connecting the film with the mom-and-pop theaters
(≤~200 screens) it documents. The company builds its distribution network *during*
production: every theater visited is a potential release partner, and each theater's
patrons are a built-in, market-by-market audience. Target: 50 screens in 50 states, each
profiled theater a premiere venue. Festival premiere strategy leads with Sundance (Jan
2028), then True/False, SXSW, and Full Frame. Secondary/right-fit platforms:
PBS/Independent Lens (especially if ITVS becomes a co-production partner), Kanopy and
Swank for educational distribution, and premium arthouse homes like MUBI or the Criterion
Channel. (**Do not** list ad-supported platforms like Tubi/Pluto for a festival-track
arthouse doc — reviewers read that as a mismatch.)

## Fundraising strategy (+ sponsorship tiers)
The strategy funds the majority of the picture through grants; once grants are secured,
the team moves to fiscal-sponsorship contracts, then crowdfunding (securing grants first
raises donor confidence). Fundraising is collaborative: Jan-David leads overall strategy,
Richard coordinates grant research and drafting, Sophia manages logistics and campaign
support — with audience built ahead of launch via social media and email so a subscriber
base is in place when campaigns open.

**Sponsorship tiers (for corporate/individual sponsors — distinct from crowdfunding
perks; see conflict note in `project-facts.md`):**
- **Bronze — $5,000:** Associate Producer credit, invite to production meetings and some
  shoot days, VIP premiere tickets, agreed percentage of grosses.
- **Silver — $10,000:** Producer credit + production-company billing, meetings and shoot
  days, VIP premiere tickets, agreed percentage of grosses and merchandise.
- **Gold — $20,000:** Executive Producer credit + top company billing, meetings and shoot
  days, VIP premiere tickets, agreed percentage of grosses and merchandise.

## Accessibility
The film will include closed captioning and a hearing-impaired audio track for D/deaf and
hard-of-hearing audiences, and a visually-impaired audio-description track for blind and
low-vision audiences.

## Work-sample description
The sample is a short documentary produced in five hours during a 2023 Theatrical
Intimacy Education intensive — written, directed, shot, and edited by Jan-David Soutar —
following an intimacy coordinator, two actors, and crew through consent-based
choreography for a vulnerable scene. It is relevant to 50 Screens twice over: it
demonstrates the observational method (small crew, natural light, subjects carrying the
material without directorial intervention), and its subject — consent and care on set —
mirrors the ethical framework 50 Screens requires when documenting owners discussing
financial vulnerability and the possibility of failure. Submitted in lieu of 50 Screens
production footage, which begins in 2026. Vimeo: https://vimeo.com/847224059.
