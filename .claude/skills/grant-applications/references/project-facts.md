## Logline
> Fifty theaters in fifty states. A portrait of the people who refuse to let the
> American movie house die, and the communities that still need it.

## Topic summary (what it covers)
Anchor theaters in four states, one on each coast/region: **California, Georgia,
Michigan, and Maryland**, plus theaters in small rural Midwestern and Southern towns.
Told from the POV of longtime patrons, theater owners and their families, programmers,
projectionists, out-of-town visitors, cinematic scholars, and council members. Driving
questions: how owners got into the business and why; what the theater was like at its
peak; first moviegoing memories; the economic waves across decades; pre/post-COVID
effects; whether family-outing traditions passed down generations; the impact of
streaming, the film-to-digital transition, and multiplexes; and why some theaters are
returning as nonprofit multi-use arts spaces — and whether that fills the void. The
underlying stakes: will local community theaters decline to the point of extinction?

## Film summary (the through-line)
A national portrait told through local stories. Each theater is a different facet of
the American experience: a single-screen cinema in a rural farming town struggling
post-pandemic; a historic downtown theater fighting rising rents in a gentrifying city;
a community-run nonprofit redefining moviegoing; and a multi-generational family
business facing succession. Structured as a road journey — episodic yet cumulative.
Seeks the tension between crisis and reinvention, not just decline.

> "Can independent theaters survive — and if so, what will they become?" The answer is
> not predetermined. 50 Screens invites the audience into the uncertainty.

## Central question (the film's spine)
Not *whether* independent theaters will survive (some will, some won't) but **what
survival costs, and who pays the price when a community institution has to change its
identity to keep existing.** The film will not pretend there's a clean answer.

## Artistic approach / style
- **Vérité, no narrator, no on-screen director.** The camera is a witness. Authority
  comes from disappearing into the spaces it documents. Direct inheritance from Barbara
  Kopple's *Harlan County USA* — "radical patience: staying still long enough for the
  truth to show up."
- **Lean crew:** 2–3 people on interview days.
- **Camera:** Sony FX3 in 4K; natural and mixed ambient light; classic single-subject
  interview grammar; medium and close framing; a second camera for reaction/context.
- **Aesthetic:** intimacy over polish; worn, specific, full of texture (light through a
  projection booth window; velvet of a well-used seat).
- **Sound is a first-class creative element:** projector hum, reel changes, the echo of
  an empty auditorium, opening-weekend crowd noise. **Original score commissioned** to
  give ambient sound room to breathe.

## Structure (three movements)
1. **Anchor theaters** — Michigan, Maryland, Georgia, California; establish the central
   question and the range of survival strategies.
2. **Regional cluster shoots** across all fifty states; a collective portrait of
   American exhibition.
3. **CinemaCon (Las Vegas)** — the industry's largest annual gathering; the contrast
   between studio spectacle and small theaters provides the film's final frame.

## Distribution — the differentiator
**Nouveau Roadshow** is a theatrical-only distribution model connecting independent
films with mom-and-pop theaters (chains of ~200 screens or fewer) across the U.S. — no
digital/streaming, physical cinema screens only. The company is **building its
distribution network during production of the film**: every theater visited becomes a
potential partner in the theatrical release, and each theater's existing audience
becomes a built-in constituency for the film. This "distribution built from the
audience outward" story is unique and should appear in every distribution/marketing
answer. A **cinema distribution database** of candidate theaters exists (NJ/MD and
beyond — owners, screens, contacts; see `sources.md`). Deal-model reference under
discussion: ~70/30 exhibitor split, 50/50 filmmaker rev share.

## Audience
Primary: adults who grew up going to neighborhood theaters (Baby Boomers, Gen X core),
designed to also reach Gen Z who never had a family-owned theater. Plus younger
cinephiles, film students, arts-community members, local advocates, and — crucially —
**the existing patron base of every theater profiled** (a built-in, market-by-market
audience via the Nouveau Roadshow theatrical release).
